Dave's Questions & Answers

Q1: "Do we get our cash back for the up front cost? If we put in $1M we just get our money back after construction?"
Short Answer: Not exactly. Here's how it works:

Your $750K–$1M in pre-construction working capital is a capital contribution to the partnership, not a loan. It doesn't get "paid back" like a loan would. Instead:

  1. At construction loan closing: The construction loan should reimburse the partnership for pre-development costs already spent. Your share of that reimbursement flows back to you — but only if the partnership agreement allows partner distributions at that stage (many don't until stabilization).
  2. Through developer fees: Your ~$952K–$1.2M cash developer fee is separate from your capital contribution. That's your compensation for being a development partner. It gets paid during/after construction per the investor-approved schedule.
  3. Through refinancing or sale: Eventually, when the property is stabilized and refinanced (or sold), your 25% equity gets a liquidation event. That's where your capital contribution gets returned + appreciation.
Capital Risk

If the project fails before construction loan closing, your pre-construction capital is at risk. If the project succeeds, you get it back through the capital waterfall — but the timeline could be 2–5 years.

Question for Seth Stratton: The partnership agreement needs to clearly define the capital account and distribution waterfall. When exactly can Fontaine recover its pre-construction capital? Is it first in the waterfall or pro-rata?

Q2: "Can I refinance separate from Trinity? Otherwise my equity is locked up."
Short Answer: Almost certainly no.

In a LIHTC partnership:

  • The partnership owns the property, not the individual partners
  • You can't put a separate mortgage on 25% of a building
  • Refinancing is a partnership-level decision that Trinity (as Managing GP) controls
  • The LIHTC investor will have restrictions on refinancing during the 15-year compliance period
  • You cannot independently refinance your partnership interest

Your Options to Unlock Equity:

  1. Sell your partnership interest — But the investor and Trinity will have right of first refusal, and there's limited market for minority interests in LIHTC deals
  2. Partnership-level refinancing — If the property appreciates and can support more debt, the partnership can refinance and distribute proceeds pro-rata. But Trinity decides when/if.
  3. After Year 15 — The LIHTC compliance period ends and the investor typically exits. That's when the real equity event happens (sale or recap).
  4. Buy/sell provision — The partnership terms include a pre-construction "buy/sell" right. This could be your exit if things aren't working.
Equity Lock-Up

Your equity IS locked up for a long time. This is typical for LIHTC deals. Plan accordingly.

Q3: "Do I get 25% of the equity in the whole deal that is 'equity'? How does the equity work?"
How LIHTC Equity Works:

The $50.3M in "tax credit equity" is NOT equity in the traditional sense. Here's the structure:

  1. A tax credit investor (syndicator) — typically a bank or financial institution — buys into the partnership for ~$50.3M
  2. In exchange, they get 99.99% of the tax credits and tax losses for 15 years
  3. They get almost zero economic interest (typically 0.01% of cash flow and residual value)
  4. Trinity + Fontaine retain the economic interest (99.99% of cash flow and residual value as the "GP interest")
  5. After Year 15, the investor exits (usually for a nominal amount like $100), and the GP partners own the building free and clear

So your 25% means:

  • 25% of the GP's share of cash flow (which is 99.99% of total cash flow) — so effectively 25% of all cash flow
  • 25% of residual value after the investor exits in Year 15+
  • 25% of developer fees
  • 25% of any refinancing proceeds
  • But NOT 25% of the $50.3M in tax credit equity — that goes to the investor in exchange for the credits
The Long Game

After Year 15, you own 25% of a stabilized, debt-reduced apartment building in downtown Worcester. If the building is worth $80–100M+ at that point (appreciated historic building, downtown location), your 25% could be worth $20–25M.

Q5: "How do tax credits work in general and here?"
Tax Credits 101:

LIHTC (Low Income Housing Tax Credit)

  • Federal program: Build/renovate affordable housing → get tax credits worth ~$0.90–$0.95 per $1 of qualified basis
  • Credits are taken annually over 10 years
  • Must keep units affordable for 15 years (compliance period) + 15 years extended use
  • A syndicator (bank) buys the credits for upfront cash (~$0.90 per $1 of credits)
  • That cash is the "equity" that pays for construction

Historic Tax Credit (HTC)

  • Federal: 20% of qualified rehabilitation expenditures on historic buildings
  • State (MA): Additional credits available
  • The $4.2M in MA Historic Tax Credits mentioned in the offer letter
  • These also get syndicated for upfront cash

In THIS Deal:

  • The $50.3M in "equity" comes from selling LIHTC + HTC to an investor
  • The investor gets the tax benefits; you get the cash to build
Tax Credit Recapture Risk

If you violate compliance (unit mix, rent limits, tenant income), you can owe back the credits. This is the "tax credit recapture" risk, and it's what the investor's guarantees protect against.

Why Guarantees Matter Here:

  • The investor will require guarantees that the project will: be completed on time, achieve stabilization, maintain compliance, have operating deficit reserves
  • These guarantees are typically "joint and several" — meaning the investor can come after EITHER partner for 100%
  • The 75/25 side agreement only governs between Trinity and Fontaine

How Tax Credits Work (CFO Reference)

The Basic Mechanism

A tax credit investor (typically a bank) pays upfront cash in exchange for tax credits that directly reduce their federal tax bill dollar-for-dollar over 10 years, plus depreciation losses over 15 years.

Math on This Deal

  • Qualified basis (construction cost on affordable units): ~$55–60M
  • LIHTC rate: ~9% annual credit for 10 years
  • Total credits generated: ~$50M
  • Investor pays: ~$50.3M upfront (at ~$0.90–$0.95 per credit dollar)
  • Investor receives over 10 years: ~$50M in direct tax bill reduction + ~$15–20M in depreciation deductions
  • Investor also receives CRA (Community Reinvestment Act) compliance credit

Why Banks Do This

  • Converts tax liability into community development (roughly break-even financially)
  • CRA compliance is required by regulators — this satisfies that requirement
  • PR/ESG value for the institution
  • The credits are dollar-for-dollar tax bill reduction, not just deductions

Why This Matters for Fontaine

  • As a developer/partner, Fontaine is on the SELLING side of credits — you create the credits, the investor buys them
  • The $50.3M in "equity" is real cash that pays for construction, funded by the investor purchasing your tax credits
  • The investor gets 99.99% of tax benefits but almost zero economic interest (0.01% of cash flow/residual)
  • After Year 15, investor exits for nominal amount ($100); GP partners retain the building
  • Understanding this structure is key to entering development — every affordable housing deal works this way

Would It Make Sense for Dave to Invest in Tax Credits Directly?

  • Probably not at this scale — requires $5M+/year in predictable federal tax liability
  • Dave's value is as the developer who CREATES credits, not as the investor who buys them
  • However: if Fontaine Bros has significant tax liability, smaller tax credit investments could be worth exploring with CFO
